Miranda rights are a set of legal warnings that must be given to criminal suspects in the United States before they are subjected to custodial interrogation. These rights stem from the Supreme Court case *Miranda v. Arizona* (1966), which ruled that the Fifth Amendment's protection against self-incrimination requires law enforcement officers to inform individuals of their rights when they are held in custody and questioned about a crime.

Here is what these rights typically include:

### 1. The Right to Remain Silent
The suspect must be informed that they have the right to remain silent. This means they do not have to answer any questions or provide any statements, as doing so could potentially lead to self-incrimination (being convicted based on their own words).

### 2. The Right to an Attorney
They must be told that they have the right to speak with a lawyer before and during questioning. If they cannot afford one, they will have the right to request an attorney appointed by the court at no cost if necessary.

### 3. Waiver of Rights
For Miranda rights to apply, the suspect must voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently waive these rights. Usually, this involves signing a form or verbally stating that they understand their rights and choose to proceed without them. If the suspect refuses to sign but still answers questions, law enforcement often records the refusal on video to prove the waiver was voluntary.

### 4. Consequences of Invocation
If a suspect invokes any of these rights (e.g., says "I want a lawyer" or "I want to remain silent"), police must immediately stop questioning and cannot continue until an attorney is present, unless they are simply booking the suspect into custody for processing purposes.

### Legal Significance
The primary purpose of Miranda warnings is to protect individuals from coercive interrogation tactics that might lead them to confess against their will or make false confessions due to fear or pressure. Without a valid waiver and proper administration of these rights, any statements obtained during custodial interrogation are generally considered inadmissible evidence in court, although they may still be used for impeachment (challenging the defendant's credibility) if the suspect takes the stand later.

In short, Miranda rights serve as a critical safeguard to ensure that criminal suspects understand their legal protections before being questioned by law enforcement.
